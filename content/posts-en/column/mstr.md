Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy / MSTR) is known as one of the largest corporate holders of bitcoin in the world.

This article explains how MSTR's bitcoin strategy works, and what risks appear when the BTC price falls.

Note: This article is not investment advice. Always check primary sources and the latest filings before making any decisions about MSTR or BTC.

# The "yield" many people misunderstand

Strategy does not pay cash dividends to its common shareholders.

The yield the company highlights is not an ordinary dividend yield. It is its own metric, **BTC Yield**.

- **Cash dividend on common stock**: none
- **BTC Yield**: a measure of how much the amount of bitcoin per share has grown

Shareholders do not receive BTC directly.

When Strategy raises capital, buys more BTC, and the BTC backing per share rises as a result, the company reports that as BTC Yield.

So rather than "a stock that automatically pays out BTC," MSTR is more accurately seen as "a stock whose company uses the capital markets to try to increase BTC per share."

# How can it keep buying BTC?

Strategy does not fund its BTC purchases from its software business alone.

It has three main sources of funding.

## Convertible notes

Convertible notes are bonds that can be converted into shares under certain conditions.

Investors keep their right to be repaid as bondholders, while having the option to convert into shares if the stock price rises.

Strategy has used this structure to raise money at relatively low interest rates and spend it on BTC.

## Selling shares on the market

MSTR's share price sometimes carries a premium over the market value of its BTC holdings.

If the company sells new shares on the market in that situation and uses the proceeds to buy BTC, it can, if things go well, increase the amount of BTC per share.

This works best, however, when the stock price is high and investor demand is strong.

## Cash flow from the core business

Strategy started out as an enterprise software company.

Cash flow from that business is one of the things supporting its bitcoin strategy.

Given the current size of its BTC holdings, though, raising money in the capital markets matters far more than operating profit.

# Strong in a bull market, reversed in a bear market

While the BTC price is rising and MSTR stock trades at a premium, the strategy looks powerful.

The company can raise money with a highly valued stock and low-interest bonds, buy more BTC, and increase BTC per share.

When the BTC price falls, however, the whole structure runs in reverse.

- Falling BTC prices reduce the value of its holdings
- The premium on MSTR stock shrinks
- Buying more BTC by issuing shares becomes harder
- Payments on bonds and preferred stock become a relatively heavier burden

What is most worrying is not the drop in BTC itself, but the loss of its ability to raise money in the capital markets.

MSTR's strategy is not just "a company that holds BTC." It is "a company that has to keep accessing the capital markets to buy more BTC."

# It won't be liquidated overnight

On the other hand, MSTR is not structured like a typical leveraged trade that gets liquidated the moment BTC falls below a certain level.

The company raises money mainly by issuing bonds, preferred stock, and common stock.

So a short-term drop in the BTC price does not immediately trigger a forced liquidation.

The trouble comes when bond maturities, preferred dividends, its ability to refinance, and the stock market's valuation all line up at once.

# Bond maturities are the real deadline

If the share price is high enough, convertible notes get converted into stock, and the burden of repaying them in cash goes away.

But if the share price is weak at maturity, investors may choose cash repayment instead of converting.

If Strategy cannot cover that with cash on hand or new financing, it faces choices like these:

- Issue new shares or bonds
- Buy back or refinance existing debt
- Use its US dollar reserve
- Sell some of its BTC

In fact, Strategy announced in May 2026 that it had repurchased $1.5 billion of its convertible notes due 2029, reducing its outstanding convertible notes from $8.2 billion to $6.7 billion.

That lowers risk, but it also shows how much this strategy depends on managing its capital structure, not only on the BTC price.

# Where things stand as of June 2026

Strategy announced that it held 843,738 BTC as of May 25, 2026.

Then an 8-K filed on June 1, 2026 was reported to show that the company sold 32 BTC between May 26 and May 31.

The sale was worth about $2.5 million, at an average price of $77,135 per BTC.

That sale is tiny compared with its total holdings: 32 BTC out of 843,738 BTC is only about 0.0038%.

The market still paid attention, because Strategy had an image of being a company that "never sells BTC."

From now on, it should be seen not only as a company that keeps buying BTC, but also as one that will sell BTC when needed to adjust its capital structure.

# What to watch when looking at MSTR

Following the BTC price alone is not enough to judge MSTR's risk.

At a minimum, you need to look at these together:

- Strategy's BTC holdings
- Average purchase price
- MSTR's premium over the value of its BTC holdings
- Convertible notes outstanding, their maturities, and BTC Par
- The burden of preferred stock dividends
- The size of its US dollar reserve
- Whether it is selling BTC
- Whether BTC Yield is actually staying positive

If BTC keeps rising, MSTR looks like a very strong machine.

But if BTC falls sharply, the stock premium disappears, and raising money gets hard, the same machine works in the opposite direction.

MSTR is not simply a bitcoin holding stock.

It needs to be understood as a fairly complex financial product that combines bitcoin, bonds, preferred stock, and a stock market premium.
